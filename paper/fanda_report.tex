\title{What Multiple-Choice TruthfulQA Rewards: Layer Contrast, Self-Evaluation and Option Length in Small
Language Models}
\author{Muhammad Naufal \quad Jeong Edon \quad Muhammad Aliff\\\small Korea University}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Multiple-choice TruthfulQA is a common test of methods that aim to make language models more truthful. We
study what it rewards, using layer contrast (DoLa), self-evaluation (P(True)) and option length, on
\mainmodel{} and seven other instruction-tuned models of 0.5 to 4 billion parameters. A variant of DoLa that led
the base model's likelihood by 0.06 MC1 on the 50 questions it was developed on falls below it on 767 held-out
questions ($-0.034$ [$-0.060$, $-0.008$]), and no layer-contrast rule beats the likelihood there. Two properties
of the scores explain such gains and losses. Under DoLa's generation settings, the plausibility constraint masks
tokens of every option, and the number of masked tokens alone decides MC1 on 73\,\% of the questions; DoLa's paper
and its released code differ on whether the constraint is used. DoLa's multiple-choice configuration cannot be told
apart from choosing the longest option (MC1 0.356 against 0.361), and on true and false options of nearly equal
length every likelihood and contrast rule is close to chance. Self-evaluation, under a design frozen before 400
confirmation questions, reaches MC1 0.623 against 0.340 for the likelihood and 0.398 for DoLa, and beats the
likelihood on 5 of 8 models; but it is as accurate without the question on 6 of 8 models, so it judges each option
as a statement rather than as an answer. We recommend four checks for multiple-choice evaluations of this kind: a
longest-option baseline, length-matched pairwise accuracy, a question-removed control, and a design frozen before
it is tested on held-out questions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Language models state falsehoods with confidence, and many methods try to reduce this; \citet{huang2025survey}
survey them. Methods that change how a model scores or decodes text are often tested on the multiple-choice version
of TruthfulQA~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}: the model scores reference true and false answers to 817 questions, and MC1
asks whether the true answer gets the highest score. DoLa, which contrasts the final layer with an earlier one,
reports large gains on this test~\citep{chuang2024dola}.

This study began as a method. We built a variant of DoLa, \method{}, on 50 development questions, where it led
the base model's likelihood on \mainmodel{}. On the other 767 questions the lead disappeared. Asking why led to an
empirical study of what multiple-choice TruthfulQA rewards. Four properties make the measurement hard:
\begin{itemize}
\item[\cha1] \textbf{Small development sets mislead.} \Method{} led the likelihood by $+0.06$ MC1 on the 50
  development questions and trails it by $-0.034$ [$-0.060$, $-0.008$] on the 767 held-out questions.
\item[\cha2] \textbf{Settings copied from generation break multiple-choice scoring.} Under DoLa's generation
  settings the plausibility constraint masks at least one token of every held-out option; on the 55\,\% of
  questions where the true option has more masked tokens, MC1 is 0.
\item[\cha3] \textbf{Option scores follow option length.} The scores of DoLa's multiple-choice configuration have a
  within-question rank correlation of $+0.669$ with option length, and choosing the longest option scores as well.
\item[\cha4] \textbf{The benchmark can be answered without the question.} A self-evaluation verdict with the
  question removed is at least as accurate as with it on 6 of 8 models.
\end{itemize}

We address them in turn. Every rule is scored on all 817 questions, and choices made on the development questions
are tested on the held-out ones (\cha1). We measure how the plausibility constraint and option length decide answers
(\cha2, \cha3). We then test self-evaluation, which asks the model whether each option is true and does not sum over
the option's tokens, under a design frozen before 400 confirmation questions, and we check it on eight models with a
question-removed control (\cha4). The report contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A held-out evaluation of DoLa in two configurations and of \method{}: no layer-contrast rule beats the base
  likelihood on MC1 on 767 held-out questions (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}).
\item Two properties that decide multiple-choice answers regardless of truth: token masking by the plausibility
  constraint and the association of scores with option length (Section~\ref{sec:mislead}).
\item A test of self-evaluation against likelihood and DoLa under a frozen design: $+0.225$ MC1 over DoLa on 400
  confirmation questions, and MC1 0.900 when only the 30\,\% most confident questions are answered
  (Section~\ref{sec:selfeval}).
\item Controls on eight models: reading the verdict from an earlier block helps on 2 of 8, self-evaluation beats
  the likelihood on 5 of 8, and the verdict is as accurate without the question on 6 of 8
  (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).
\item Four checks we recommend for multiple-choice evaluations of truthfulness (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Contrastive and layer-contrastive decoding.}
Contrastive decoding scores each candidate token by the difference between the log-probabilities of a large
(expert) and a small (amateur) language model, and keeps only tokens whose expert probability is at least $\alpha$
times the expert's top probability (an \emph{adaptive plausibility constraint}, $\alpha =
0.1$)~\citep{li2023contrastive}. DoLa applies the same idea inside one model: it contrasts the final (mature)
layer with an earlier (premature) layer read through the output head, chooses the premature layer at every token
as the candidate with the largest divergence from the mature layer, and uses the same
constraint~\citep{chuang2024dola}. DoLa also describes DoLa-static, which uses one premature layer chosen on a
validation set, and reports large TruthfulQA gains for LLaMA models~\citep{chuang2024dola}. We do not propose a
decoding method. We re-evaluate DoLa in two configurations and one variant, \method{}, that we developed on 50
questions, on 767 held-out questions, and we measure two properties of the scores that explain their gains and
losses: token masking by the constraint and the association with option length (Sections~\ref{sec:heldout}
and~\ref{sec:mislead}).

\paragraph{TruthfulQA and its weaknesses.}
TruthfulQA has 817 questions written so that a common misconception gives a plausible false answer; its
multiple-choice task ranks reference true and false answers by the model's likelihood~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}.
Two later reports question the multiple-choice format. A simple decision tree over the answer options, using
heuristics such as ``eliminate answers which imply other answers'' and ``select the odd answer out'', reaches 79.6\,\%
in theory~\citep{turner2025gaming}, and the dataset's authors note that the format may measure test-taking ability
rather than truthfulness and recommend a binary-choice version with one true and one false
option~\citep{evans2025improved}. Both concern how the options relate to each other. The two properties we report
are different: likelihood and contrast scores follow the length of each option (\cha3), and each option, judged on its
own, is about as easy to label without the question as with it (\cha4).

\paragraph{Partial-input baselines.}
A model that solves a task from part of its input shows that the dataset leaks the label. In natural language
inference, classifiers that see only the hypothesis predict the label well above the majority
class~\citep{gururangan2018annotation,poliak2018hypothesis}. In multiple-choice question answering, a prompt with
the answer choices and no question beats a majority baseline in 11 of 12 dataset--model
pairs~\citep{balepur2024artifacts}. Our question-removed control differs in one respect: every option is scored in
its own forward pass, so the model cannot compare the options and must judge each one as a statement.

\paragraph{Self-evaluation.}
Language models can be asked whether a proposed answer is true and the probability of the reply ``True'',
P(True), read as a score; for answers the model sampled itself, this score showed encouraging performance and
calibration that improved with model size~\citep{kadavath2022language}. Self-evaluation scores have since been used to decide when to answer
(selective generation, evaluated on TruthfulQA and TL;DR)~\citep{ren2023selfevaluation}, to guide beam search in
multi-step reasoning~\citep{xie2023selfevaluation}, and to label a model's own responses as training data for
factuality, with DoLa among the baselines~\citep{zhang2024selfalignment}. We use the self-evaluation score directly to
rank the fixed options of multiple-choice TruthfulQA, compare it with likelihood and DoLa under a design frozen
before 400 confirmation questions, and repeat the comparison with likelihood on eight models of 0.5 to 4 billion
parameters (Sections~\ref{sec:selfeval} and~\ref{sec:controls}).

\paragraph{Truthfulness in hidden states and intermediate layers.}
A classifier trained on hidden-layer activations predicts whether a statement is true~\citep{azaria2023internal}.
Decoding intermediate layers through the output head shows that, with false demonstrations in the prompt, accuracy
falls after a ``critical layer''~\citep{halawi2024overthinking}. We read the self-evaluation verdict from
intermediate blocks in the same way, without training a probe, and test whether an intermediate block beats the
final layer on eight models (\emph{early exit}, Section~\ref{sec:controls}).

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
A classifier that may abstain trades coverage, the share of inputs it answers, against the risk on the answered
inputs (the risk--coverage trade-off)~\citep{elyaniv2010foundations}; for deep networks, a threshold on the
confidence can be set so that the risk stays below a chosen level with high probability~\citep{geifman2017selective}. We set three thresholds
on the exploration questions and report the accuracy they give on the confirmation questions
(Section~\ref{sec:selfeval}).

\section{Scoring Rules}
\label{sec:rules}

Every rule scores each answer option $y = (y_1, \dots, y_T)$ of a question separately, from one teacher-forced
forward pass, and MC1 chooses the option with the highest score (Figure~\ref{fig:rules}). Let $x$ be the prompt,
$h^{(j)}_t$ the hidden state after transformer block $j$ at the position that predicts $y_t$, and $\phi$ the model's
final normalisation followed by its output head. Block $j$ then gives the logits $z^{(j)}_t = \phi(h^{(j)}_t)$ and
the distribution $q_j(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}) = \operatorname{softmax}(z^{(j)}_t)$; $N$ is the final block.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/f1_scoring}
\caption{The three ways to score one answer option. The likelihood and DoLa scores read the option's own tokens
after a prompt that asks the question; the self-evaluation score reads one reply token after a prompt that shows
the option and asks whether it is true. Our likelihood prompt says ``answer options'' but does not list them
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). The question-removed control drops the line ``Question: \{question\}'' from the
self-evaluation prompt.}
\label{fig:rules}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Base likelihood.}
The standard multiple-choice score of TruthfulQA is the option's log-likelihood under the base
model~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa},
\begin{equation}
  S(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log q_N(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}),
  \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the chat-formatted question. Every token adds a negative term, so the sum favours short options; we
also report the per-token score $S(y)/T$, which removes this dependence on the number of tokens (\cha3).

\paragraph{DoLa.}
DoLa selects the premature layer at every token,
\begin{equation}
  M_t = \arg\max_{j \in \mathcal{J}} d\bigl(q_N(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t}),\, q_j(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})\bigr),
  \label{eq:dola-select}
\end{equation}
from a set $\mathcal{J}$ of candidate blocks, and scores token $v$ by
\begin{equation}
  F_t(v) =
  \begin{cases}
    \log q_N(v \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log q_{M_t}(v \mid x, y_{<t}) & \text{if } v \in \Vhead,\\
    -1000 & \text{otherwise,}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:dola-score}
\end{equation}
where the adaptive plausibility constraint keeps the tokens
\begin{equation}
  \Vhead = \bigl\{v : q_N(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \ge \alpha \max_w q_N(w \mid x, y_{<t})\bigr\},
  \qquad \alpha = 0.1~\text{\citep{chuang2024dola}}.
  \label{eq:vhead}
\end{equation} The DoLa paper calls $d$ the Jensen--Shannon divergence; its reference
implementation, which we reproduce, computes $d(q_N, q_j) = \tfrac12[\KL(m \Vert q_N) + \KL(m \Vert q_j)]$ with
$m = \tfrac12(q_N + q_j)$, averaged over the vocabulary, which places the mixture first in both terms. DoLa's
reference code uses different settings for free generation and for TruthfulQA multiple choice, and we report both:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{DoLa (generation settings)}: the constraint of Equation~\eqref{eq:vhead}, the renormalised option score
  \[ \textstyle \sum_t \log \operatorname{softmax}(F_t)(y_t), \]
  and candidate blocks from the first quarter of the network (blocks 1, 3, \dots, 9 of the 36 in \mainmodel{}).
  Our development experiments used these settings.
\item \emph{DoLa (MC configuration)}: no constraint, the plain sum without renormalisation,
  \[ \textstyle \sum_t \bigl[\log q_N(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log q_{M_t}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\bigr], \]
  and candidate blocks from the second half of the network. DoLa used layers 16, 18, \dots, 30 of the 32-layer
  LLaMA-7B; we transfer them by relative depth to blocks 18, 20, \dots, 34 of \mainmodel{}.
\end{itemize}
The two sources differ on the constraint in multiple choice. The DoLa paper states that for TruthfulQA it replaces
$-\infty$ with $-1000$ in the constraint ``to avoid ruining LM likelihood scores''~\citep{chuang2024dola}, which
implies the constraint is applied; the released multiple-choice script sets $\alpha = 0$ by default, which keeps
every token, and its published TruthfulQA commands do not change it. We follow the released script.
Neither candidate set was tuned for \mainmodel{}. The constraint matters for multiple choice because the option's
tokens are fixed in advance: an option token outside $\Vhead$ contributes about $-1000$ to the option score
whatever the contrast says (\cha2, Section~\ref{sec:mislead}).

\paragraph{\Method{}.}
This is the variant we developed on the 50 development questions, and the held-out evaluation tests it
(\cha1). It changes four settings of DoLa:
\begin{enumerate}
\item no plausibility constraint;
\item the difference of raw logits in place of log-probabilities, with the option score renormalised as in DoLa's
  generation settings,
  \[ \textstyle \sum_t \log \operatorname{softmax}\bigl(z^{(N)}_t - z^{(L)}_t\bigr)(y_t); \]
  without the constraint this gives the same option scores as the log-probability difference, because the two
  differences differ by a constant per position that the softmax removes;
\item layer selection by the standard Jensen--Shannon divergence $\JSD(p, q) = \tfrac12[\KL(p \Vert m) + \KL(q
  \Vert m)]$ of the two distributions at temperature $\tau = 0.5$, in place of the reference implementation's
  divergence at $\tau = 1$, a setting inherited from earlier code and not tested on its own;
\item one premature layer $L$ per question, selected at the first answer position and kept for all later tokens.
\end{enumerate}
The first answer position follows the prompt alone, which is the same for every option, so all options of a
question share the layer $L$. To test the fourth change, we also re-select the layer every $k$ tokens, $k \in \{1,
4\}$; \method{} is $k = \infty$. Its candidate blocks are those of DoLa's generation settings.

\paragraph{Self-evaluation, P(True).}
Following \citet{kadavath2022language}, the model is shown the question and one option and asked whether the
option is true, with the user message
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Question: \{question\}\\ Proposed answer: \{option\}\\ Is the proposed answer true? Reply with one word: True or
False.
\end{quote}
The score is the log-odds of the reply tokens at the first reply position,
\begin{equation}
  V(y) = \log q_N(\texttt{True} \mid x_y) - \log q_N(\texttt{False} \mid x_y) = z^{(N)}(\texttt{True}) -
  z^{(N)}(\texttt{False}),
  \label{eq:selfeval}
\end{equation}
where $x_y$ is the prompt for option $y$. It needs one forward pass per option and does not sum over the option's
tokens, so, unlike Equation~\eqref{eq:likelihood}, it has no term per token. MC2 uses $V$ in place of the
log-likelihood. A second wording (``\{question\}\textbackslash n\textbackslash nAnswer: \{option\}\textbackslash
n\textbackslash nIs this answer correct? Reply with one word: Yes or No.'') is an ablation.

\paragraph{Early exit of the verdict.}
The same log-odds can be read after an earlier block $j$, from $z^{(j)} = \phi(h^{(j)})$ at the first reply
position. On \mainmodel{} we use the mean over blocks 27--30 of 36, chosen on the exploration questions; on the
other models the block is chosen on 50 labelled questions and tested on the rest (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).

\paragraph{Verdict with the question removed.}
As a partial-input control (\cha4), we delete the line ``Question: \{question\}'' from the self-evaluation prompt,
so that the model judges the option as a statement. We also tested one way to use it: subtracting the
question-removed verdict from the verdict, which removes what the option alone gives away, as pointwise mutual
information does for likelihoods. The protocol for this test was fixed before any question-removed score existed.

\paragraph{Longest option.}
To measure \cha3, the baseline chooses the option with the most characters. MC1 counts a tie between a true and
a false option as wrong; token counts tie more often than character counts, so we count characters
(Section~\ref{sec:mislead} reports both).

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
With the self-evaluation score, a question is answered only if the difference between the two highest scores among
its MC1 options reaches a threshold. The three thresholds are the differences at 25\,\%, 50\,\% and 75\,\% coverage on
the exploration questions; they are then fixed and applied to the confirmation questions.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Data.}
We use the multiple-choice version of TruthfulQA, 817 questions with 5,882 distinct answer
options~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}. Each question has one set of options with exactly one true answer (on average
5.04 options), which MC1 uses, and a second set with several true and false answers, which MC2 and MC3 use.

\paragraph{Question sets.}
Every choice in this study was made on a named subset of the questions, and the main results are reported on
questions that played no part in it (Figure~\ref{fig:sets}); where a result uses the questions a choice was made on,
its section says so. The \emph{development set} is 50 questions drawn with seed 42;
\method{} and all settings of the earlier experiments were chosen on it. The \emph{held-out set} is the other 767
questions: we scored all 817 questions once with every likelihood and contrast rule and removed the development
questions at analysis time. For self-evaluation, the 817 questions were split once, with seed 20261115, into 417
\emph{exploration} questions (the 50 development questions and 367 others) and 400 \emph{confirmation} questions. The
self-evaluation design, including the early-exit blocks and the abstention thresholds, was chosen on the
exploration questions and written to a file with its hash before the confirmation questions were scored once. The
likelihood and DoLa scores of the held-out questions existed before this split, but no self-evaluation score of a
confirmation question did.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/f2_question_sets}
\caption{The question sets, drawn to scale, and what was decided on each. Choices about layer contrast were made on
the 50 development questions and tested on the 767 held-out questions; choices about self-evaluation were made on
the 417 exploration questions and tested on the 400 confirmation questions.}
\label{fig:sets}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Metrics.}
MC1 is 1 if the true option of the first set scores strictly above every false option, so a tie counts as wrong.
MC2 is the probability mass on the true options of the second set after a softmax over the option scores; with the
log-likelihood as score, this is the score of the TruthfulQA paper, ``the total normalized likelihood of the true
answers''~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}. MC3 is the share of true options that score above the best false option. We
report all three, as DoLa does~\citep{chuang2024dola}, and use MC1 as the primary metric, because it is the
decision a user of the scores would make. To separate a rule's preference for true answers from its preference for
long ones (\cha3), we also report \emph{length-matched pairwise accuracy}: over the pairs of one true and one false
option of the second set whose token counts differ by at most one, the share of pairs in which the true option
scores higher (a tie counts one half), averaged over questions. We defined this measure for this study; it is not a
standard metric.

\paragraph{Statistical tests.}
Two rules are always compared on the same questions. For each difference we report the mean, a 95\,\% percentile
interval from a paired bootstrap over questions (10,000 resamples, seed 0)~\citep{bergkirkpatrick2012empirical}
and, for MC1, the numbers of questions gained and lost with an exact McNemar test~\citep{mcnemar1947note}, a
choice of tests that follows \citet{dror2018hitchhiker}. Within each table, the bootstrap and McNemar $p$-values
of all comparisons are adjusted together by the Holm--Bonferroni method; the comparison of self-evaluation with
likelihood on eight models (Section~\ref{sec:controls}) uses unadjusted intervals, as its protocol fixed in advance.
For scale: a single MC1 has a 95\,\% interval of about $\pm 0.033$ on the 767 held-out questions and of about $\pm
0.12$ to $\pm 0.14$ on the 50 development questions (Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}).

\paragraph{Models.}
The main model is \mainmodel{}~\citep{qwen2024qwen25}, on which all rules were developed and tested. The
cross-model checks add seven instruction-tuned models chosen by a rule written into their protocol: decoder language models
of at most 4 billion parameters that run in bfloat16 on one 12\,GB GPU, have a chat template and answer directly,
because the verdict is read at the first reply token. These are Gemma~3 4B and 1B~\citep{gemmateam2025gemma3},
Falcon3-3B~\citep{tii2024falcon3}, Qwen2.5-1.5B and 0.5B~\citep{qwen2024qwen25},
Phi-3.5-mini~\citep{abdin2024phi3} and SmolLM2-1.7B~\citep{benallal2025smollm2}, all in their instruction-tuned
versions. Llama~3.2 3B was planned and left out, because access to its gated repository was not granted;
SmolLM2-1.7B took its place. The protocol of the early-exit check was fixed after the first three models
(\mainmodel{}, Gemma~3 4B, Falcon3-3B) had been scored and before the other five.

\paragraph{Prompts.}
All prompts use each model's chat template with a single user message. For the likelihood and DoLa scores, the
user message is the question followed by ``Answer with one of the provided answer options only.'', and the
option's tokens follow the assistant prefix. The options are not listed in the prompt, so the instruction refers
to options the model does not see; we kept this prompt for every run so that all likelihood and contrast scores stay
comparable. The TruthfulQA paper scores options after a fixed question-answering prompt of trivia questions and
recommends it for comparisons~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}; because we use a different prompt, our absolute scores are
not comparable with published ones, and we compare rules only with each other on the same prompt. The
self-evaluation prompts are given in Section~\ref{sec:rules}.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
All runs reported here used PyTorch 2.9.1 and Transformers 4.57.6 on one NVIDIA RTX 5070 (12\,GB), with \mainmodel{} in bfloat16,
the precision of its configuration. The likelihood and contrast scorer processes one option at a time and is limited
by the CPU: scoring all nine likelihood and contrast rules on the 817 questions took 3.2 hours for \mainmodel{}, and
the base likelihood alone took 6.5 to 11.5 minutes per model. Self-evaluation scores the options in batches of
eight and took 66 seconds for the 400 confirmation questions, both wordings and every block included. Each run
records the code version it ran with. Rerunning the development questions in the current environment reproduced
the earlier development runs to within two questions per rule on MC1 (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\section{Layer Contrast on Held-Out Questions}
\label{sec:heldout}
This section answers \cha1: does a layer-contrast rule that led on the development questions also lead on
questions it was not chosen on? All four rules scored the 817 questions in one run, and Table~\ref{tab:heldout}
splits the result into the 50 development and the 767 held-out questions.

rule with the base likelihood on the held-out questions: mean MC1 difference with its 95\,\% paired-bootstrap
interval, questions gained and lost, and the exact McNemar $p$-value, Holm-adjusted over the 20 $p$-values of the
five comparisons of this test family; the other two compare \method{} with each DoLa configuration.}
\label{tab:heldout}

\end{table}

\paragraph{No layer-contrast rule beats the base likelihood on held-out questions.}
On the development questions, \method{} and DoLa in its multiple-choice configuration led the base likelihood by
0.06 and 0.08 MC1. On the held-out questions neither lead remains: DoLa (MC configuration) is $+0.023$ [$-0.020$,
$+0.068$], and \method{} is below the base likelihood, $-0.034$ [$-0.060$, $-0.008$], although not after the Holm
adjustment (Table~\ref{tab:heldout}). DoLa with its generation settings is the only rule below the base likelihood
on all three metrics with intervals that exclude 0. \Method{} also falls below DoLa (MC configuration) on the held-out questions, by
$-0.057$ [$-0.100$, $-0.012$] MC1 (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.156$). The development intervals are four times wider
than the held-out ones (Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}): with 50 questions, a lead of 0.06 MC1 is inside the noise of a
single score, and choosing among variants on those questions can select for that noise.

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Of the four changes in \method{}, only removing the constraint changes the scores.}
Table~\ref{tab:factorial} (Appendix~\ref{app:tables}) adds the changes one at a time, starting from DoLa with its generation settings.
Replacing the log-probability difference by the raw-logit difference while the constraint is kept changes nothing
measurable. Removing the constraint leaves MC1 unchanged, $+0.009$ [$-0.020$, $+0.039$], and raises MC2 and MC3 by 0.046 and
0.033 (Holm-adjusted $p$ 0.061 and 0.020).
Fixing the premature layer once per question and switching to the standard Jensen--Shannon divergence then changes
MC1 by $+0.004$ [$-0.013$, $+0.021$]. The variant without the constraint is still below the base likelihood on
MC1, by $-0.038$ [$-0.064$, $-0.012$] (Holm-adjusted $p = 0.094$).

\paragraph{How often the layer is re-selected does not matter on held-out questions.}
Re-selecting the premature layer at every token ($k = 1$), every four tokens or once per question gives the same
held-out scores within $0.008$ MC1, while the development MC1 differed by 0.06 to 0.08 between $k = 1$ and the other two
(Table~\ref{tab:interval}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). Appendix~\ref{app:stability} states when a longer interval leaves the decisions unchanged;
the held-out result is consistent with that condition holding for most questions, which we did not measure.

\section{Why Multiple-Choice Scores Mislead}
\label{sec:mislead}

The held-out run also explains why the development results misled. Two properties of the scores, both measured
on the 767 held-out questions, decide many multiple-choice answers without regard to truth.

\paragraph{The plausibility constraint decides most answers under DoLa's generation settings (\cha2).}
Under the generation settings, every one of the 5,529 held-out options has at least one token outside the plausible
set (4.01 on average). Each such token adds $-1000$ to the option score, while the rest of the score lies between
$-84.2$ and $0$, so whenever two options differ in the number of masked tokens, the one with fewer wins. This
count alone decides MC1 on 73\,\% of the questions: where the true option has more masked tokens than some false
option, MC1 is exactly 0, and where it has fewer, MC1 is exactly 1 (Table~\ref{tab:masking}). Of the 90 held-out
questions that the base likelihood answers correctly and DoLa (generation settings) does not, 40 are of the first
kind. A setting that limits the next token in free generation thus ranks fixed options by how many of their tokens
the final layer finds implausible. The DoLa paper describes the constraint as part of its multiple-choice
evaluation, while its released script turns it off (Section~\ref{sec:rules}), so which of the two one copies
decides the result.

\paragraph{Likelihood and contrast scores follow option length (\cha3).}
Within a question, the base likelihood prefers short options (mean rank correlation with length in characters
$-0.285$), as expected of a sum of negative log-probabilities, and DoLa (MC configuration) prefers long ones
($+0.669$; Table~\ref{tab:length}). Choosing the
longest option, which needs no model, reaches MC1 0.361; DoLa (MC configuration) reaches 0.356, and the two cannot
be told apart: $-0.005$ [$-0.038$, $+0.027$], 86 questions gained and 90 lost. Comparing only true and false options
of nearly equal length removes this effect: every likelihood and contrast rule then picks the true option in 46 to
54\,\% of the pairs, close to the 50\,\% of a random choice. DoLa's multiple-choice gain over the base likelihood is
therefore consistent with a preference for long options, which on TruthfulQA are the true option more often than a
random choice would be (longest option 0.361, shortest 0.172 MC1), rather than with better recognition of true
answers.

\paragraph{Mixing the contrast into the likelihood does not help.}
We also scored $S_{\text{base}} + \beta S_{\text{contrast}}$, the base likelihood plus $\beta$ times the DoLa (MC
configuration) score, for 16 values of $\beta$ from 0 to $\infty$, and chose $\beta$ on the development questions
by a rule fixed before the held-out scores were read (highest MC1, then MC3, then MC2, then the smallest $\beta$).
The chosen $\beta = 10$ is indistinguishable from DoLa alone on the held-out questions, $+0.003$ [$-0.005$, $+0.010$]
MC1.

\section{Self-Evaluation}
\label{sec:selfeval}

Self-evaluation asks the model whether each option is true instead of reading the option's likelihood
(Section~\ref{sec:rules}). Its design, including the early-exit blocks and the abstention thresholds, was frozen on
the 417 exploration questions and tested once on the 400 confirmation questions (\mainmodel{}).

\paragraph{Self-evaluation beats every likelihood and contrast rule.}
On the confirmation questions, self-evaluation reaches MC1 0.623, against 0.340 for the base likelihood and 0.398
for DoLa (MC configuration) (Table~\ref{tab:selfeval}). Every MC1 difference has a Holm-adjusted $p$ below 0.001,
and the MC2 and MC3 differences are positive in every comparison (Table~\ref{tab:selftests}). The score is higher on
the confirmation questions than on the exploration questions it was designed on (0.571), so we see no sign that the
design overfitted the exploration questions.

\paragraph{The gain is not a length effect.}
The self-evaluation score has a rank correlation of $+0.069$ with the option's token count, against $-0.281$ for
the base likelihood and $+0.791$ for DoLa (MC configuration), and it picks the true option in 73\,\% of the
length-matched pairs, where the likelihood and contrast rules stay between 52 and 54\,\% (Table~\ref{tab:selfeval}).

\paragraph{Reading the verdict earlier helps on this model; a second wording works too.}
Averaging the verdict over blocks 27--30 of 36, chosen on the exploration questions, adds $+0.062$ [$+0.035$,
$+0.092$] MC1 on the confirmation questions. A second wording (``Is this answer correct? Reply with one word: Yes
or No.'') reaches MC1 0.578 and also beats the per-token likelihood (Table~\ref{tab:selftests}). Section~\ref{sec:controls}
tests whether the early-exit gain holds on other models.

\paragraph{Selective prediction.}
The gap between the two highest option scores ranks the questions by how likely the answer is to be right
(Figure~\ref{fig:riskcoverage}). With the three thresholds frozen on the exploration questions, answering 30\,\% of
the confirmation questions gives MC1 0.900 [0.843, 0.950] on them (Table~\ref{tab:selective}). The thresholds were
set for 25, 50 and 75\,\% coverage and gave 30, 60 and 82\,\% on the confirmation questions, so the share of
answered questions moves between question sets even when the accuracy holds.

\section{Controls on Eight Models}
\label{sec:controls}

Three checks on the 417 exploration questions ask how far the results of Section~\ref{sec:selfeval} reach beyond
\mainmodel{}, and what the verdict uses (\cha4). Each check's protocol was written down before the scores it
judges existed, except that the early-exit protocol was written after three of the eight models had been scored.

\paragraph{The early-exit gain does not generalise.}
For each model, 50 exploration questions are drawn as a labelled set, the block in the upper half of the network
with the highest MC1 on them is chosen, and the gain over the final layer is measured on the other 367 questions,
over 500 draws. A model shows the gain if the mean gain is positive and fewer than 5\,\% of the draws lose; early exit
would count as a general improvement if 6 of the 8 models showed it. Two do, \mainmodel{} and Falcon3-3B, and none
of the five models scored after the protocol was written (Table~\ref{tab:earlyexit}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}). The by-block curves suggest
why (Figure~\ref{fig:earlyexit}): on Qwen2.5-1.5B, Gemma~3 4B and Phi-3.5-mini, for example, the verdict reaches its
final level in the middle of the network and stays there, so no later block is better than the final layer.

\centering
\input{figures/f5_early_exit}
\caption{MC1 of the self-evaluation verdict read after each block, eight models, 417 exploration questions; depth 1
is the final layer. The dashed line is the model's base likelihood on the same questions. On all eight models
the verdict at the final layer is above the base likelihood; Qwen2.5-3B and Falcon3-3B peak before the final layer.}
\label{fig:earlyexit}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The verdict works as well without the question (\cha4).}
With the question deleted from the prompt, the verdict's MC1 is at least as high as with it on 6 of 8 models
(Table~\ref{tab:questionremoved}, Appendix~\ref{app:tables}; the two were not tested against each other). Every option is scored in its own
forward pass, so the model cannot compare options: it judges each option as a statement. On TruthfulQA the options
are statements that can be judged alone, and we read the result as showing that the self-evaluation score measures
mostly whether the model takes the statement to be true, not whether it answers the question. We also
tested one use of the question-free verdict, fixed before it was scored: subtracting it from the verdict, as
pointwise mutual information does for likelihoods. This loses on all 7 counted models, with every interval below 0,
and we reject it. Adding the two verdicts instead gains on 3 of 7 models; it was not part of the protocol's
decision and is reported only.

\paragraph{Self-evaluation beats the likelihood on five of eight models.}
We scored the base likelihood of all eight models with the code of the held-out run (it reproduces that run's
scores of \mainmodel{} exactly) and compared it with self-evaluation by a rule fixed before any of these scores
existed: self-evaluation beats likelihood scoring on a model if its MC1 is higher than both the sum and the
per-token likelihood, with both 95\,\% intervals above 0. This holds for 5 of the 8 models (Table~\ref{tab:likelihood}).
On the other three, Qwen2.5-0.5B (whose verdict is at chance), Gemma~3 1B and SmolLM2-1.7B, the difference from the
per-token likelihood is inside the noise. Two of the five wins rest on intervals that only just exclude 0
(Falcon3-3B and Qwen2.5-1.5B, lower bounds $+0.012$ and $+0.014$, unadjusted McNemar $p$ 0.024 and 0.018); the rule
fixed no multiple-comparison adjustment. For \mainmodel{} these are the questions its design was chosen on; its
clean test is Section~\ref{sec:selfeval}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What the multiple-choice scores measure.}
For the likelihood and contrast rules, a multiple-choice score mixes three things: whether the model prefers the true
statement, how long each option is, and settings such as the plausibility constraint that interact with the number
of tokens. On 50 questions, differences of 0.02 to 0.08 MC1 between such rules are inside the noise of a single
score (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), and on 767 questions they mostly disappear (Section~\ref{sec:heldout}). Once option
length is controlled, none of these rules separates true from false options much better than chance
(Section~\ref{sec:mislead}). Self-evaluation is different in size and in kind: its gain is large, it holds on
questions it was not designed on, and it survives the length control. What it measures, however, is whether the
model takes each option to be true as a statement; on TruthfulQA this is about as easy without the question as with
it (Section~\ref{sec:controls}).

\paragraph{Recommended checks.}
Each finding suggests a check that costs little and could be reported with any multiple-choice result on this
benchmark:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A longest-option baseline}, counted in characters, because token counts tie and MC1 counts ties as
  wrong (\cha3).
\item \emph{Length-matched pairwise accuracy} next to MC1, MC2 and MC3, so that a gain from preferring long options
  is visible (\cha3).
\item \emph{A question-removed control} that scores each option without the question, to see how much of the score
  needs the question (\cha4).
\item \emph{A design frozen before held-out questions}, tested once with paired tests, and a statement of the exact
  settings used, including the plausibility constraint (\cha1, \cha2).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the results do not show.}
We tested multiple-choice scoring only. The results say nothing about DoLa in free generation, where the
plausibility constraint was designed to act, nor about the larger LLaMA models of the DoLa paper. That self-evaluation
works without the question also does not mean it fails where the question matters; on TruthfulQA the options happen
to be statements that can be judged alone.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One benchmark, multiple choice only.} All results are on TruthfulQA's multiple-choice task; we did not
  test open-ended generation or the binary-choice version~\citep{evans2025improved}.
\item \textbf{Small models.} All models have at most 4 billion parameters, against 7 to 65 billion in the DoLa paper.
\item \textbf{Our prompt.} The likelihood prompt refers to answer options it does not list, and it is not the
  question-answering prompt of the TruthfulQA paper, so absolute scores are not comparable with published ones
  (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
\item \textbf{Layer contrast on one model.} DoLa and \method{} were scored on \mainmodel{} only; their candidate
  layers were transferred by relative depth and not tuned for it.
\item \textbf{Self-evaluation confirmed on one model.} Only \mainmodel{} has scored confirmation questions; the other
  seven models were checked on the exploration questions, and the likelihood comparison there uses no
  multiple-comparison adjustment.
\item \textbf{Order of the analyses.} The likelihood and DoLa scores of all held-out questions existed before the
  questions were split for self-evaluation, and the early-exit protocol was written after three of the eight models
  had been scored.
\item \textbf{Two wordings.} How much self-evaluation depends on the wording of its prompt was not studied beyond the
  two wordings tested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On multiple-choice TruthfulQA with small models, self-evaluation beats likelihood and layer-contrast scores by a
wide margin on questions it was not designed on, while the gains of layer contrast on 50 development questions do
not hold on 767 held-out questions. Two properties of the scores, token masking by the plausibility constraint and
the association with option length, explain much of how likelihood and contrast rules win and lose on this
benchmark, and the self-evaluation verdict works about as well without the question. Each of these findings turns
into a check that future evaluations can report (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

Two directions follow. First, the checks can be applied to other decoding and scoring methods evaluated on this
benchmark, and to larger models. Second, self-evaluation should be tested where the question is needed to judge an
answer: open-ended generation, the binary-choice version of TruthfulQA~\citep{evans2025improved}, and short-answer
question answering.

\section{When a longer selection interval changes nothing}
\label{app:stability}

This appendix states when re-selecting the premature layer less often leaves the decisions unchanged. It does not
show that a longer interval is better: the reference is per-token selection ($k = 1$) itself.

Let $L^\star_t$ be the layer that per-token selection would choose at step $t$ and $L^k_t$ the layer in use with
interval $k$. Write $g^\star_t = z^{(N)}_t - z^{(L^\star_t)}_t$ and $g^k_t = z^{(N)}_t - z^{(L^k_t)}_t$. Their
difference $e^k_t = g^k_t - g^\star_t = z^{(L^\star_t)}_t - z^{(L^k_t)}_t$ does not depend on the mature layer.

\paragraph{Next token.} Let $a = \arg\max_v g^\star_t(v)$ be unique, with margin
$m_t = g^\star_t(a) - \max_{v \ne a} g^\star_t(v)$. If $2\lVert e^k_t \rVert_\infty < m_t$, then
$\arg\max_v g^k_t(v) = a$, since $g^k_t(a) - g^k_t(v) \ge m_t - 2\lVert e^k_t \rVert_\infty > 0$ for every $v \ne a$.

\paragraph{Option ranking.} For any vectors $x, x'$ and index $i$,
$\lvert \log\operatorname{softmax}(x)_i - \log\operatorname{softmax}(x')_i \rvert \le 2 \lVert x - x' \rVert_\infty$,
because $\log\operatorname{softmax}(x)_i = x_i - \operatorname{logsumexp}(x)$ and both terms are 1-Lipschitz in
$\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$. Summing over the tokens of an option $y$ gives
$\lvert S_k(y) - S_\star(y) \rvert \le 2 \sum_t \lVert e^k_t \rVert_\infty$. Hence, if
$S_\star(y) - S_\star(y') > 2 \sum_t \lVert e^k_t(y) \rVert_\infty + 2 \sum_t \lVert e^k_t(y') \rVert_\infty$, the
interval-$k$ decoder ranks option $y$ above $y'$, as per-token selection does.
